FRONT|Introduction and method
FRONT|This edition treats Cairo and Giza as one integrated historical, urban and cultural landscape, not as a list of famous sights or a one-day programme. It covers archaeological sites, museums, palaces, districts, religious sites, markets, crafts, the artistic scene, nature, food, entertainment and nearby trips.
FRONT|The encyclopedia does not include ticket prices or service prices, and it does not confirm opening hours, because that information changes. At the end of the file it lists official and encyclopedic links so that readers can verify and update details before publishing any version or confirming any booking.
FRONT|Source method: UNESCO and official Egyptian bodies are the first reference for heritage and operational information. Wikipedia and encyclopedic indexes are used to discover sites, names, categories and cross-links, and then important information is checked against specialised sources. Wikipedia is not a final reference for visiting times, opening status or prices.

CH|1|Geography and understanding Greater Cairo
P|Cairo is not just an administrative centre. It is the heart of a large urban region in which Cairo Governorate connects with Giza Governorate and neighbouring urban areas on both banks of the Nile. The historic core lies east of the river, while the Giza Plateau and the city of Giza lie to the west. For that reason, it is best to plan visits by area, not by the order in which the sights are listed.

P|Do not combine the Pyramids, Saqqara, Islamic Cairo and East Cairo in one day. Traffic, walking, security checks, the time spent inside sites and rest breaks make grouping by geography essential. CH|3|The Giza Pyramids plateau and the Sphinx
SEC|3.1|Giza Plateau – Giza Plateau
P|The plateau contains royal funerary complexes from the Fourth Dynasty, the best known of which are the pyramids of Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure. The pyramid is not a standalone building; it is part of a system that includes the valley temple, the causeway, the mortuary temple, the tombs of the royal family and the elite, and sometimes smaller pyramids and boat pits. Reading the site as a funerary city reveals its religious, political and social functions [m4].
P|The Great Pyramid – Khufu / Great Pyramid
P|The largest of the Giza pyramids, and the last surviving of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. The official site of the antiquities authority states that its original height was about 146.5 metres, and that its interior contains the Grand Gallery and internal chambers, including the King's Chamber. Its structure also reveals the complexity of transport, casing, stone-cutting and engineering work [m5].
P|Pyramid of Khafre – Pyramid of Khafre
P|It sometimes looks the tallest visually because of its relatively elevated position and because its summit retains part of the original casing. It is linked to the valley temple, the causeway and the Sphinx, which makes it a key axis for understanding the layout of the funerary complex.
P|Pyramid of Menkaure – Pyramid of Menkaure
P|The smallest of the three main pyramids. Remains of granite use are visible at its base alongside limestone. It is linked to queens' pyramids and a mortuary temple.
P|Great Sphinx – Great Sphinx
P|A huge statue with a lion's body and a human head, carved from the natural bedrock and contextually linked to the Khafre complex. It is one of the most famous symbols of ancient Egypt. Hypotheses about the dating of some parts of the area or the statue's function should be presented as research debates where there is disagreement, not as settled facts.
P|Other elements on the plateau
P|Valley temple of Khafre: a station linked to rituals of receiving the royal body.
P|Causeway: a ceremonial corridor connecting the valley temple with the mortuary temple.
P|Queens' pyramids: smaller structures linked to the royal family.
P|Mastabas and tombs: burial places for senior officials, relatives and the elite.
P|Workers' tombs and the village linked to construction: they help in understanding the community of workers, administration and supply.
P|Boat pits: linked to beliefs and royal funerary rituals.
P|Excavation areas: they should not be assumed to be open to visitors merely because they appear on the map.
P|How is a pyramids tour built?
P|The experience can be divided into the outside view and panorama, an explanation of the elements of the funerary complex, and then an optional entry inside when one of the pyramids is open. Internal passages can be narrow, steep and hot, so they do not suit every visitor. Do not promise entry before confirming the ticket and the actual availability.

CH|4|Memphis Necropolis: Saqqara, Memphis, Dahshur and Abu Sir
P|UNESCO describes "Memphis and its Necropolis – the Pyramid Fields from Giza to Dahshur" as a wide archaeological system including pyramids, rock tombs, mastabas, temples and ancient settlements. It records the development of funerary architecture from the mastaba to the step pyramid and then the smooth-sided pyramid [m6].
SEC|4.1|Saqqara – Saqqara
P|Saqqara is one of the most important necropolises of Memphis, and is regarded as an open-air museum of the development of funerary architecture. Its best-known monument is the Step Pyramid of Djoser and its complex. It also contains pyramids from the Fifth and Sixth Dynasties and tombs decorated with scenes of daily life. The official site also lists the Imhotep Museum and the remains of the Monastery of Saint Jeremiah as components of the site [m7].
P|Step Pyramid of Djoser: an important architectural shift in building with stone, linked to the name of the architect Imhotep.
P|Djoser's funerary complex: a wall, ritual buildings and symbolic elements surrounding the pyramid.
P|The South Tomb: part of the complex; access depends on operations.
P|Pyramid of Unas: one of the most famous sites of the Pyramid Texts inside the burial chamber.
P|Pyramid of Teti: linked to tombs of senior officials in the area.
P|Mastaba of Mereruka: one of the decorated tombs that reveal scenes of daily life.
P|Tomb of Ti: an important example of relief sculpture and painting.
P|Tombs of Kagemni and the nobles: examples of social life and administrative status.
P|The Serapeum: galleries for burying the sacred Apis bulls, linked to the worship of Ptah in Memphis; the official site explains its function and history [m8].
P|Imhotep Museum: a stop for understanding the finds and the history of excavation in Saqqara.
P|Monastery of Saint Jeremiah: evidence of the continued use of the area in the Coptic period.
SEC|4.2|Memphis – Memphis / Mit Rahina
P|Memphis was one of the most important capitals of ancient Egypt and a centre for the worship of the god Ptah. Its archaeological site lies near Mit Rahina and includes an open-air museum and huge pieces, the most famous of which is the statue of Ramesses II. UNESCO links Memphis with the chain of necropolises stretching from Giza to Dahshur [m6].
SEC|4.3|Dahshur – Dahshur
P|Dahshur is an essential stop for understanding the development of the pyramid. King Sneferu chose the area to build the Bent Pyramid and then the Red Pyramid, one of the earliest true pyramids with a complete shape. The area also contains Middle Kingdom pyramids, including the Black Pyramid, with clear variation in preservation and access [m9].
P|The Bent Pyramid: shows the adjustment of the angle of slope during construction, and is a witness to an engineering experiment.
P|The Red Pyramid: a pyramid with complete straight faces, and one of the largest pyramids in Egypt.
P|Middle Kingdom pyramids: architectural remains of varying condition, not all of them prepared for general public visits.
P|The desert setting: gives the visitor a different feeling from the crowds of the Giza plateau.
SEC|4.4|Abu Sir, Abu Ghurab, Zawiyet el-Aryan and Abu Rawash
P|These sites complete the picture of the Memphis necropolis and reveal the variety of royal architecture and the rituals of sun worship and burial. They are of high value to specialists, but they are not all treated as sites open to the public every day. Record the state of access, roads and permits before including them in a programme.
SEC|4.5|Suggested visiting order
P|Saqqara first, with a limited number of tombs and internal entrances.
P|The Imhotep Museum if it is open on the chosen ticket.
P|Memphis / Mit Rahina to visit the open-air museum.
P|Dahshur if time and weather allow, after checking whether entry to the pyramid is available.

CH|6|Islamic Cairo: Fatimid, Mamluk and Ottoman
P|Historic Cairo has been on UNESCO's World Heritage List since 1979. Its value lies in the survival of a connected urban fabric: streets, markets, gates, walls, houses, wakalas, sabils, schools, mosques and cemeteries, not in individual buildings alone. UNESCO points to the layers of Fustat, al-Askar, al-Qata'i', Fatimid Cairo and the Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman extensions [m2].
SEC|6.1|Fatimid Cairo, its walls and gates
P|Bab al-Futuh: a northern gate that survives from the walls of Fatimid Cairo.
P|Bab al-Nasr: a defensive northern gate showing features of military architecture.
P|Bab Zuweila: one of the best-known historic gates of Cairo in the south.
P|Al-Azhar Mosque: a mosque and scholarly centre founded in the Fatimid period, which became an educational institution of global influence.
P|Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah Mosque: one of the leading Fatimid monuments at the northern edge of historic Cairo.
P|Al-Aqmar Mosque: a distinctive stone façade on Muizz Street.
P|Al-Salih Talai Mosque: a surviving Fatimid structure near Bab Zuweila.
SEC|6.2|Muizz Street, Gamaleya and Hussein
P|Muizz Street is the main axis of Fatimid Cairo. The experience lies in reading the façades, entrances, domes, minarets, sabils and wakalas, and noticing how the architecture is linked to trade, housing, education and worship.
P|Complex of Sultan Qalawun: an architectural complex of artistic and historic value, including religious and charitable elements.
P|Complex of Sultan Barquq: a school, mosque and mausoleum on Muizz Street.
P|Complex of Sultan al-Ghuri: religious, commercial and cultural buildings in the heart of historic Cairo.
P|Al-Suhaymi House: a historic house that shows the courtyard, the mashrabiya screens and the internal reception hall.
P|Zeinab Khatoun House, Al-Harawi House and Sitt Wasila House: examples of Cairo's residential architecture; check the visit programmes.
P|Wikalat al-Ghuri: a historic commercial building and a cultural space that sometimes hosts heritage shows.
P|Egyptian Textile Museum: located in Sabil Muhammad Ali near Bayn al-Qasrayn.
P|Al-Hussein Mosque and its courtyard: a religious and social centre close to the Khan and al-Azhar.
P|Khan al-Khalili: a historic market from the Mamluk period, with specialised markets, cafés and crafts.
SEC|6.3|Darb al-Ahmar and Bab Zuweila
P|Darb al-Ahmar is a living neighbourhood where Mamluk and Ottoman buildings sit alongside workshops, homes and markets. It helps show that historic Cairo is not a closed museum but a city that remains alive.
P|Mosque of Sultan al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh: near Bab Zuweila, with its two minarets above the gate.
P|Qijmas al-Ishaqi Mosque: one of the masterpieces of late Mamluk architecture.
P|Aq Sunqur Mosque / the Blue Mosque: distinguished by its blue ceramic tiles and decoration.
P|Mosque of Amir Shaykhu and his complex: a monument of the Bahri Mamluk period.
P|Sabil and Kuttab of Umm Abbas: a water and educational structure from the nineteenth century on Salibah Street.
P|Khayamiya workshops: a traditional craft that makes embroidered fabrics and coloured decoration.
SEC|6.4|Salibah Street and Sayyida Zeinab
P|Salibah Street is one of the most important axes of Islamic Cairo. It runs between the Citadel Square and Sayyida Zeinab Square and is lined with mosques, schools, houses, sabils and charitable buildings. The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities describes it as an open-air museum of Islamic architecture [m11].
P|Mosque of Ahmad ibn Tulun: one of the oldest mosques to survive in its historic form, with a large courtyard and a spiral minaret.
P|Qaraita House / Gayer-Anderson Museum: a group of Ottoman houses beside Ibn Tulun.
P|Mosque and school of Amir Shaykhu: from the Bahri Mamluk period.
P|Sultan Hassan Mosque: a school and a huge mosque from the fourteenth century, and one of the most important examples of Mamluk architecture.
P|Al-Rifa'i Mosque: a nineteenth-century building that is linked to the tombs of the Alawite dynasty.
P|Sayyida Zeinab Mosque: one of the best-known popular religious centres in Cairo.
SEC|6.5|The Citadel of Saladin and its surroundings
P|The Citadel of Saladin, on the Muqattam heights, was a centre of government and defence for centuries. It contains mosques, museums, historic buildings and views over the city. Its most important elements include the Mosque of Muhammad Ali, the Mosque of al-Nasir Muhammad, the Mosque of Sulayman Pasha, al-Gawhara Palace, the Police Museum and the Military Museum [m12].
SEC|6.6|Historic cemeteries – the Qarafa
P|Cairo's cemeteries are not one area; they include the Great Qarafa, the cemetery of Imam al-Shafi'i, the cemetery of Qaitbay and the Northern Cemetery. They contain domes, tombs, mosques and the tombs of historical figures from different eras. Among their best-known elements are the Complex of Sultan Qaitbay, the Dome of Imam al-Shafi'i, the Complex of Sultan Barquq in the desert, and the tombs of princes and historic families.
P|Note: these areas combine heritage with homes and cemeteries in social use. Do not enter private buildings or passages that are not open to visitors, and do not photograph residents without permission. Check the availability of each site and respect religious and funerary privacy.

CH|7|Old Cairo and the Coptic and Jewish heritage
P|The Old Cairo and Fustat area is one of the most important zones of religious and heritage diversity in Cairo. It contains the remains of Babylon Fortress, a group of churches and monasteries, the Coptic Museum, Ben Ezra Synagogue, the Mosque of Amr ibn al-As, the remains of the city of Fustat and the Nilometer on Roda Island [m13].
SEC|7.1|Babylon Fortress and the Religions Complex
P|Babylon Fortress: a Roman fortress linked to the area around which ancient Egypt's development grew.
P|The Hanging Church: the Church of the Virgin Mary, which sits above one of the fortress's towers / gates and is known for its wooden and iconic elements.
P|Church of Abu Sarga: a historic church linked to the church tradition of the Holy Family's journey to Egypt.
P|Church of St George: one of the most prominent churches within the fortress area.
P|Church of St Barbara: a historic church within the old churches zone.
P|Church of Abu Sifein: dedicated to Saint Mercurius and known by this popular name.
P|Coptic Museum: displays Coptic art and the development of Egyptian Christian culture.
P|Ben Ezra Synagogue: an important site in the history of the Jewish community in Egypt; check the visiting rules.
P|Mosque of Amr ibn al-As: linked to the founding of Fustat and the beginnings of Islamic architecture in Egypt.
SEC|7.2|Other Coptic sites in Cairo
P|St Mark's Cathedral in Abbassia: a modern church seat with religious and historical importance.
P|Church of St Simon the Tanner in Muqattam: a church complex carved into the mountain in the Muqattam area.
P|Churches of Haret Zuweila: a group of Coptic churches with a long history.
P|Churches of Shubra, Heliopolis and Zeitoun: they reflect the expansion of the Coptic community into modern Cairo's districts.
P|Sites linked to the Holy Family's journey: the story should be presented as church tradition, and actual access should be checked.
SEC|7.3|Jewish heritage
P|Alongside Ben Ezra Synagogue in Old Cairo, Cairo has landmarks linked to modern Jewish history, including the Temple of the Samaritans in downtown and other sites that may be closed or have limited access. Do not assume that all places of worship are open to general tourism; contact the responsible body and obtain prior approval where needed.
SEC|7.4|Etiquette for religious visits
P|Respect prayer times, masses and religious occasions.
P|Wear suitable clothing; you may be asked to take off your shoes or cover your head.
P|Do not photograph prayers or people without permission.
P|Do not touch icons, antiquities, textiles or religious objects.
P|Do not present a site as open to the public without confirmation from the responsible administration.

P|Zeinab Khatoun House, Al-Harawi House and Sitt Wasila House: heritage houses in the al-Azhar and Muizz area; visit programmes and events vary.
P|Wikalat al-Ghuri and Wikalat Qaitbay: examples of architecture for trade, lodging and storage.
P|Sabils and kuttabs: service buildings that provided water and education, and which are common in historic Cairo.
P|Historic baths: some buildings survive or are known historically, but not every historic bath is open or suitable for general visits.
SEC|8.3|Khedivial Cairo and downtown
P|Downtown, or Khedivial Cairo, is a nineteenth- and early twentieth-century urban extension west of the historic city. It has wide streets, squares, apartment blocks and façades with European and local influences. Academic studies show that Cairo's suburbs, such as Garden City, Zamalek, Maadi and Heliopolis, were formed through different planning experiences, not one single pattern [m14].
P|Tahrir Square and its surroundings: a modern urban knot and a political and historic landmark.
P|Talaat Harb Street: commercial façades and buildings from the early twentieth century.
P|Mustafa Kamel Square, Abdeen Square and Opera Square: points for understanding Khedivial planning.
P|Downtown apartment blocks: their balconies, façades, entrances, staircases and old lifts.
P|Institution, bank, newspaper and theatre buildings: part of the modern history of the city.
P|Cafés and arcades: they help in reading the cultural and social life of Cairo.
SEC|8.4|Modern architecture and civic landmarks
P|Cairo Tower: a modern landmark on Zamalek Island with panoramic views over the Nile and the city.
P|Cairo Opera House: a cultural and artistic centre on the Gezira grounds; its current building opened in 1988.
P|October War Panorama: a museum and documentary exhibition about the October War in Nasr City.
P|Unknown Soldier Memorial: a national and memorial site in Nasr City.
P|Cairo University: buildings of historic and educational value in Giza, subject to entry rules.
P|Radio and Television Building and Maspero: part of the history of media and modern urban development on the Nile.
P|Major sports venues: part of event tourism when there is a match or a tournament.

CH|17|Practical guide for visitors, organisers and drivers
SEC|17.1|Transport
P|The metro: useful for some routes; its suitability depends on the location of the hotel and the destination. Check the official line information before relying on a specific route [m16].
P|Private cars and taxis: flexible when visiting distant sites, but the travel time is affected by traffic.
P|Buses and shared transport services: may suit some trips; coverage and availability vary.
P|Walking: best inside heritage areas after choosing a reasonable distance; pavements may be uneven or crowded.
P|Nile boats: a viewing or entertainment trip, depending on the operator and the landing point.
SEC|17.2|Weather and clothing
P|Outdoor visits are usually more comfortable in the mild periods of the year. Summer months need an early start, water, a hat, sunscreen and shaded breaks. In winter it can get cold in the evening. Follow the weather forecast before the trip.
SEC|17.3|Photography
P|Personal and commercial photography may be subject to different rules at archaeological sites and museums.
P|Pregnant women, lighting, flash or photography inside some halls may be prohibited.
P|Photographing residents, workers and children needs clear permission.
P|Drones are subject to special rules and permits, so do not promise their use.
P|Do not touch antiquities or climb onto a building or wall that is not designated for that, in order to get a photo.
SEC|17.4|Safety and comfort
P|Look after your belongings and keep a copy of the hotel details and your passport.
P|Agree in advance on the car, the meeting point, the nature of the service and any extra charges.
P|Drink from a safe water source and take breaks on long walking days.
P|Do not enter tunnels, tombs, closed buildings or areas that are not authorised.
P|Follow the instructions of the security staff and employees at archaeological and religious sites.
SEC|17.5|Accessibility
P|Some archaeological sites have uneven floors, stairs and narrow passages, so do not assume that every site suits wheelchairs or pushchairs. Ask the operator about alternative entrances, toilets, seating, shaded areas and lifts, and specify the level of walking required before confirming the programme.
SEC|17.6|Operational guidance for a tourist transport company
P|Confirm the client's name, contact number, hotel, meeting point and departure time before the day of the visit.
P|Clarify whether the price includes the car, the driver, fuel, waiting time, road and parking fees, tickets and the guide.
P|Explain in writing the length of the day, the distance, the charges for extra time or distance, and the entry costs.
P|Do not guarantee the opening of a site, a tomb or an internal pyramid before checking the operation and tickets.
P|Do not present the driver as a licensed archaeological guide unless he holds the appropriate licence.
P|Use a licensed tourist guide when the client needs specialised archaeological explanation.
P|Avoid pressuring the tourist to buy products, ride camels or pay tips.
P|Send a written plan of the stops, rest points and meeting place after each visit.
P|Record the driver's name, the car number and the contact and emergency details.

CH|19|Glossary of historical and architectural terms

P|Simple meaning
H|Necropolis

H|Mastaba
P|A rectangular ground tomb with sloping sides and a flat roof.
H|Valley temple

H|Causeway

H|Mortuary temple
P|A temple for rituals and offerings linked to the deceased king.

P|A tower linked to the call to prayer that became a prominent feature of Islamic architecture.
H|Mihrab
P|A niche that marks the direction of the qibla in a mosque.
H|Madrasa
P|A religious educational institution, which may be part of an architectural complex.
H|Sabil
P|A charitable structure providing drinking water, which may have a kuttab above it.
H|Kuttab
P|A traditional place for teaching children the Quran, reading and writing.
H|Wakala / khan
P|A commercial building for lodging merchants and storing goods.
H|Khanqah
P|A building linked to Sufism, residence, worship and teaching.
H|Mashrabiya
P|A projecting wooden element that provides ventilation, privacy and control of light.
H|Tomb dome
P|An architectural covering above a tomb or shrine.
H|Qarafa
P|A historic name for Cairo's cemeteries and funerary areas.
H|Muqarnas
P|Transitional geometric decorative elements resembling stepped niches.
H|Mosaic
P|An art of assembling small units to form images or decoration.
H|Urban heritage
P|The group of buildings, streets, squares and activities that shape the identity of a historic place.
H|Nature reserve
P|A protected area that preserves biological, geological or landscape diversity.

P|Closing notes and later development
P|This encyclopedia provides broad thematic and geographic coverage, but it is not a final inventory of every monument or building in Greater Cairo. Historic Cairo alone contains a large number of small structures that are not all suitable for general visits. Some sites are closed, under restoration, or require a permit or special coordination.
P|The appropriate next stage is to build a spatial database for each site, including: the Arabic and English names, the category, the district, the description, the period, the importance of the visit, an approximate duration, ease of access, the official website link, the map link, the verification status and the date of the last review. Licensed photos, maps and interactive links can then be added.
P|There are no ticket prices in this edition. If they are added in future, they should be placed in a separate dated appendix, with the date of the last check, so that they are not mixed with the more stable historical information.
